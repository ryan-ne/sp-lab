## B16 · Administrative distance and route preference

**Why this day exists**
B19's loop forms *because* of administrative distance — eBGP at 20 beats OSPF at 110, so a route that left your IGP comes back in through BGP and wins. You cannot build that loop deliberately, or fix it, without today. It is also the first time you meet a concept that every vendor names differently, which matters at B20 and D20.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* why administrative distance cannot prevent a redistribution loop, naming the property it lacks.

**Source — 35 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 15 min | Default distance values across vendors — build the table, do not just read it | FRR: https://docs.frrouting.org/en/latest/zebra.html · Junos route preference: https://www.juniper.net/documentation/ |
| 10 min | Why eBGP is 20 and iBGP is 200 — the reasoning, not the number | any BGP reference |
| 10 min | Junos calls it **preference**, Cisco calls it **administrative distance**, and the defaults differ. Note every mismatch | vendor docs |

**Do — 55 min**

1. Build the comparison table yourself before reading anyone else's: rows = connected, static, eBGP, OSPF intra, OSPF external, IS-IS, iBGP; columns = Cisco / Junos / FRR. Fill from memory first, then correct. **The corrections are the study list.**
2. On the B13 topology, advertise the same prefix into OSPF *and* as a static route on the same router. `show ip route` — one wins. Confirm which, and why, from your table.
3. Change the static's distance above OSPF's. Watch the winner flip. Now you have seen preference decide, not the protocol.
4. **The insight that matters:** administrative distance is *local to the router*. It is not carried in any protocol message, no neighbour sees it, and two routers can disagree about it completely. Say that out loud — it is the whole answer to the objective, and it is why B18's tags exist.
5. Set contradictory distances on two adjacent routers for the same prefix and watch the routing become inconsistent between them. That inconsistency is a two-router preview of the B19 loop.

**Cheapest hands-on**
Steps 2–3 alone are fifteen minutes on a lab that already exists. Do not let this become a reading day.

**Done when**
- [ ] Three-vendor distance table complete, built from memory then corrected
- [ ] Winner flipped by changing distance, observed in the RIB
- [ ] Can state, unaided, that distance is local and uncarried — and why that makes it useless against a loop

**Cards touched** — CP-20 (first half)

## B17 · Route-maps and prefix-lists

**Why this day exists**
This is the syntax every remaining policy day is written in. CP-19 asks where the implicit deny bites differently in redistribution than in BGP filtering, and that difference has bitten every network engineer at least once, usually in production.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* what happens to a route that matches no clause of a route-map, and why the consequence differs between a redistribution statement and a BGP neighbour filter.

**Source — 35 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 15 min | FRR route-map reference — `match`, `set`, sequence numbers, `on-match` / `continue` | https://docs.frrouting.org/en/latest/routemap.html |
| 10 min | Prefix-lists and the `ge` / `le` semantics. Work out what `10.0.0.0/8 ge 24 le 24` matches before reading the answer | https://docs.frrouting.org/en/latest/filter.html |
| 10 min | RFC 8212 — skim only. It is B32's day, but knowing eBGP now defaults to announcing nothing reframes what a filter is *for* | https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc8212 |

**Do — 55 min**

1. **Prefix-list drill.** Write one that matches `10.0.0.0/8` and all subnets between /24 and /28, but *not* the /8 itself. Then test it: generate ten prefixes on either side of every boundary — /23, /24, /28, /29, the /8 — and confirm each is matched or not as you predicted. Getting `ge`/`le` wrong is the most common filtering bug there is, and the only cure is having tested the boundaries once.
2. Build a three-sequence route-map: sequence 10 permits and sets a metric, 20 denies a specific prefix, 30 permits everything else. Predict the fate of five test prefixes, then run it.
3. **Now remove sequence 30.** Predict again. Everything not matched by 10 or 20 disappears — the implicit deny. Confirm it in the RIB.
4. Apply the same route-map two ways and note the different blast radius:
   - to a `redistribute` statement — the implicit deny silently drops routes from entering the protocol
   - to a BGP neighbour `route-map out` — the implicit deny silently stops announcing them
   Same rule, two very different outages. **This is CP-19.**
5. Write down the sequence-numbering convention you will use for the rest of the plan — increments of 10, room to insert. Use it consistently from here; D41's ingress framework is large enough that ad-hoc numbering becomes unmanageable.

**Cheapest hands-on**
Step 1 is the whole value. Even if the rest slips, do the boundary testing.

**Done when**
- [ ] Prefix-list boundary behaviour verified against test prefixes at /23, /24, /28, /29 and the /8
- [ ] Three-sequence route-map behaving exactly as predicted for five prefixes
- [ ] Implicit deny demonstrated, and its two different consequences described in writing
- [ ] Sequence-numbering convention written in the journal

**Cards touched** — CP-19

## B18 · Redistribution — metric translation, seed metrics, loops, tags

**Why this day exists**
Tomorrow you build the loop. Today you predict it on paper, so that tomorrow is verification rather than discovery. A loop you predicted and then observed teaches; a loop that surprises you just gets fixed by trial and error.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* what a route tag carries that administrative distance does not, and why that difference is what breaks the loop.

**Source — 40 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 15 min | Seed metrics — what happens when you redistribute with no metric set, per target protocol. OSPF, IS-IS and BGP all behave differently, and one of them silently drops the routes | FRR docs per protocol |
| 15 min | Metric translation across protocol boundaries: an OSPF cost, an IS-IS metric and a BGP MED are not the same unit and do not convert | any reference |
| 10 min | Route tags — a 32-bit value carried *in the protocol*, settable and matchable | FRR route-map `set tag` / `match tag` |

**Do — 50 min**

1. Single-point redistribution first: OSPF into BGP on one router. Look at what metric arrived on the far side. Then do it with no metric specified and find the default. **One of your target protocols will drop the routes entirely without a seed metric** — find out which, today, on a two-node lab, rather than at D19.
2. Redistribute in both directions at that one point. Confirm nothing bad happens. Single-point mutual redistribution is safe, and knowing *why* is the setup for step 3.
3. **Predict the two-point loop on paper.** Two routers, both redistributing OSPF↔BGP. Trace one prefix: it originates in OSPF, leaves via router 1 into BGP, crosses the AS, arrives at router 2, and is redistributed back into OSPF. Now ask what OSPF's route-selection does with it. Write the answer down before tomorrow. Use your B16 distance table — the answer is in it.
4. Set a tag on redistribution: `set tag 100`. Confirm the tag is present in the *receiving* protocol's database. This is the property AD lacks — the tag travels with the route, so a second router can see where it came from. **That is the objective.**
5. Write the fix before you have the problem: a route-map that denies re-redistribution of anything tagged 100. Have it ready for tomorrow.

**Cheapest hands-on**
Step 4. Ten minutes, and it is the day's whole point.

**Done when**
- [ ] Seed-metric default identified for each target protocol, including the one that drops routes
- [ ] Two-point loop predicted **in writing**, with the distance values that cause it
- [ ] A tag set on redistribution and observed surviving into the other protocol's database
- [ ] The tag-deny route-map written and ready

**Cards touched** — CP-20, CP-21

## B19 · LAB — mutual redistribution at two points, build the loop, fix it

*Run this on the 180-minute Saturday slot — see the scheduling note at the top.*

**Why this day exists**
The block's deliverable, and the first genuine failure you engineer rather than stumble into. The discipline is the same one D13, D27 and D95 use: predict, break, observe, fix, verify. Today it costs you nothing. At D95 you do it to a five-AS network.

**Objective**
A demonstrated routing loop caused by mutual redistribution at two points, and a tag-based fix, both with evidence committed.

**Source — 15 min**
No new reading. Re-read your own B18 prediction. If you cannot restate it before deploying, that is the day's first finding — write it down.

**Do — 150 min**

1. **Build it.** OSPF domain with at least four routers. Two of them (`asbr1`, `asbr2`) also run BGP to an external AS. Both redistribute OSPF→BGP and BGP→OSPF. Topology and startup configs committed to `01-igp-core/` — configs in the repo, not typed at the console, per the A2 standard in [`docs/artifacts.md`](../docs/artifacts.md).
2. **Observe the damage.** Pick a prefix that originates inside OSPF. Follow it: on `asbr2`, does it now appear as an OSPF *external* learned via BGP, rather than as the internal route it really is? Check `show ip route` and the OSPF database on a router that should only ever have known it internally.
3. Capture the evidence properly — the RIB entry showing the wrong protocol or the wrong next-hop, and the path a traceroute actually takes. **The screenshot is the artifact.** Recreating it later costs a session.
4. Confirm the mechanism against B16: which distance value made the redistributed route win over the native one? Say it out loud. If the answer surprises you, B16 did not stick.
5. **Fix it with tags.** Tag on redistribution out, deny on redistribution back in, at *both* ASBRs. Not one — the asymmetry of fixing only one side is itself instructive if you have five minutes.
6. Verify: the prefix is back to being an internal OSPF route everywhere, and the traceroute is sane again. Re-run step 2's exact commands and diff the output against the captures from step 3.
7. **Then try to fix it the wrong way.** Raise OSPF's external distance above eBGP's instead of using tags. It appears to work. Write down why it is fragile — it depends on every router agreeing about a value that is not carried anywhere, which is exactly what B16 step 4 told you.
8. Journal: the prediction, what actually happened, and where they differed.

**Done when**
- [ ] Four-plus-node topology with two redistribution points, deploying clean from one command, configs in the repo
- [ ] Loop or suboptimal path **demonstrated with captured output**, not asserted
- [ ] The responsible distance value named
- [ ] Tag fix applied at both ASBRs and verified by diffing against the broken-state capture
- [ ] The distance-based "fix" tried, and its fragility written down
- [ ] Prediction-vs-reality difference recorded in the journal

**Cards touched** — CP-19, CP-20, CP-21 — all three, exercised together

## B20 · The same policy in Junos

**Why this day exists**
MX and PTX dominate service provider networks, and D20/D21 assume you can read and write Junos policy. More than that: Junos policy syntax *forces* the mental model — named terms, explicit `then accept`/`then reject`, and per-protocol default policies you have to know rather than absorb. Writing yesterday's policy again in a second syntax is how you find out which parts you understood and which you copied.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* Junos's default export policy for BGP, and for OSPF, and why they differ.

**Source — 30 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 15 min | `policy-statement` structure — terms, `from`, `then`, and evaluation order | https://www.juniper.net/documentation/ |
| 10 min | Default routing policies per protocol. **This is the day's objective — Junos will not advertise what you assume it advertises** | Juniper TechLibrary, routing policy defaults |
| 5 min | `show | compare`, `commit confirmed`, `rollback` | https://www.juniper.net/documentation/ |

**Do — 60 min**

*If vJunos would not run on your hardware at B6*, use the fallback you wrote in the journal that day — cEOS for the syntax-comparison exercise is a poor substitute, so prefer the CML free tier or an hourly cloud VM. Do not skip the day; the plan names Junos in six places and D21 depends on it.

1. Translate yesterday's tag-deny policy into a `policy-statement`, term by term. Keep both files side by side and commit them together — the pair is worth more than either alone, and it is a portfolio piece in miniature.
2. Map the vocabulary explicitly, in a table you write: route-map sequence → term; `match` → `from`; `set` → `then`; implicit deny → **the protocol's default policy, which is not the same thing**.
3. **Find the difference that matters.** In FRR, a route-map with no matching clause denies. In Junos, an unmatched route falls through to the *protocol's default policy*, and that default differs between BGP and OSPF. Determine both by experiment, not by reading — advertise nothing explicitly and see what appears at the neighbour.
4. Apply a policy, then `show | compare` before committing. Then `commit confirmed 2` and let it roll back on purpose. Feel the safety net; it is the single best habit Junos teaches, and it is why D39's config work will be less frightening than it should be.
5. Chain two policy-statements on one export and confirm evaluation order. Chaining is how D20 and D41 build anything non-trivial.

**Done when**
- [ ] Yesterday's policy expressed in both syntaxes, both committed side by side
- [ ] Vocabulary mapping table written
- [ ] Junos default export policy for BGP **and** OSPF determined by experiment, and the difference explained
- [ ] `commit confirmed` allowed to roll back at least once
- [ ] Two chained policy-statements with evaluation order confirmed

**Cards touched** — CP-19 (re-armed in a second syntax), CP-30 (armed — `commit confirmed` is B39's card, but you have now met it)
