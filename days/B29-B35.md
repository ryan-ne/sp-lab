## B29 · Path selection, from memory

**Why this day exists**
This is the highest-frequency question in service provider interviews, and [`docs/checkpoints.md`](../docs/checkpoints.md) seeds **CP-02 today** — the one card that never retires, re-asked every 30 days regardless of streak. D23 repeats this day at carrier depth. Everything in B30 is an application of what you memorise now.

**Objective**
*Say out loud, in order, with tie-breakers, unaided:* the BGP best-path selection algorithm. Three times, on three separate sheets, without checking between attempts.

**Source — 30 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 15 min | The full ordered list, one vendor's version. FRR's is a good baseline because it is close to the RFC | https://docs.frrouting.org/en/latest/bgp.html |
| 10 min | RFC 4271 §9.1 — the decision process as specified, in three phases. Note how much of what vendors do is *not* in the RFC | https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc4271#section-9.1 |
| 5 min | Junos vs Cisco differences — Junos has no weight, and the tie-breaks at the bottom differ | vendor docs |

**Do — 60 min**

1. Write the list. Then write it again an hour later. Then again at the end of the session. **Three separate attempts, no peeking between them** — the repetition is the day, not the reading.
2. For every step, write *why it is at that position*. The order is not arbitrary: locally-configured policy beats anything a neighbour told you, which is why weight and local-pref come first; the things you cannot influence come last. An ordered list you can recite but not justify falls apart under a follow-up question.
3. Mark which steps are **local to your router**, which are **AS-wide**, and which are **influenced by a neighbour**. Three colours. This grouping is what makes CP-03 — why inbound TE is weak — obvious rather than memorised, and you met it by experiment at B28.
4. **The tie-breakers are the differentiator.** Anyone can say "shortest AS path". Get to the bottom of the list: oldest eBGP route, lowest router ID, shortest cluster list, lowest neighbour address. Most candidates stop at MED; getting past it is what the question is actually testing.
5. Note the vendor deltas explicitly. Cisco's weight does not exist in Junos. Junos's `active` route selection has its own vocabulary. FRR sits closer to the RFC. **Say which vendor's list you just recited** — an interviewer who runs Junos will notice if you recite Cisco's and cannot say so.
6. Log CP-02 honestly in the answer log. `n` unless all three attempts were complete and correct. A generous `y` here is the single most expensive dishonesty available in this repo.

**Cheapest hands-on**
On the B25 lab, `show bgp <prefix>` and read the reason line the implementation gives for its choice. Confirm it against your list.

**Done when**
- [ ] The algorithm written three times, unaided, on separate sheets
- [ ] Each step justified by *why* it sits at that position
- [ ] Steps colour-grouped: local / AS-wide / neighbour-influenced
- [ ] Tie-breakers below MED written out in full
- [ ] Vendor deltas noted, and the vendor of your recited list named
- [ ] CP-02 logged, honestly

**Cards touched** — **CP-02** (first attempt, never retires)

## B30 · Lab — win the path four different ways

**Why this day exists**
Yesterday's list becomes real today. The point is not that you can change the winner — it is that you can say *which step of the algorithm* each change hits, which is what turns memorisation into understanding.

**Objective**
The same prefix, learned from two sources, won four different ways — with the algorithm step responsible named for each.

**Source — 15 min**
No new reading. Re-read your own B29 list. If you cannot reproduce it before deploying, repeat B29's step 1 now and let the lab run short.

**Do — 75 min**

1. Extend the B25 topology so one prefix is learned from two different external ASes. Confirm which wins by default, and — before changing anything — **predict the step responsible**. It will be AS_PATH length or, if those are equal, something further down that you should be able to name.
2. **Win it with LOCAL_PREF.** Set it higher on the loser. Confirm the flip. Step 2 of the list.
3. Revert. **Win it with AS_PATH.** Prepend on the current winner until it loses. Note how many prepends it took, and that this is the lever your *neighbour* uses on you. Step 4.
4. Revert. **Win it with ORIGIN.** Change one side's origination method so its ORIGIN code differs — `network` gives `i`, `redistribute` gives `?`. Confirm `i` beats `?`. Step 5, and it is the step people forget exists.
5. Revert. **Win it with MED** between two links from the same neighbouring AS. Step 6, and confirm it only applies when the neighbouring AS is the same — that condition is why MED is weak.
6. For each of the four, record: what you changed, which step decided it, and **whether a neighbour could have overridden you**. That last column is CP-03's answer written out as data.
7. Now the useful failure: set LOCAL_PREF on the route you want to *lose*. Nothing happens the way you hoped, because local-pref is evaluated before AS_PATH and simply wins. Understanding that a high-priority lever cannot be undone by a low-priority one is what stops policy from becoming guesswork at D41.
8. Commit topology and the four-way table to `02-bgp-policy/`.

**Done when**
- [ ] Default winner predicted correctly before any change
- [ ] Four separate manipulations, each reverted before the next
- [ ] The responsible algorithm step named for each
- [ ] The override column filled in — who else could beat you, and with what
- [ ] Table committed to `02-bgp-policy/`

**Cards touched** — CP-02 (exercised), CP-24 (re-armed)

## B31 · Communities

**Why this day exists**
The A3 community scheme at D32–D34 is the flagship artifact's core, and [`docs/community-scheme.md`](../docs/community-scheme.md) is already stubbed out waiting for it. Today is where the mechanism becomes concrete. CP-26 asks what forces you to large communities — a question with a precise answer that most people fudge.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* why a 4-byte ASN cannot express a useful `ASN:value` tag in a standard community, and what large communities change.

**Source — 40 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 15 min | RFC 1997 — standard communities, 4 octets, and the well-known values: NO_EXPORT, NO_ADVERTISE, NO_EXPORT_SUBCONFED | https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc1997 |
| 10 min | RFC 8092 — large communities, 12 octets as three 4-byte fields. Read the motivation section; it is the answer to today's objective | https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc8092 |
| 10 min | RFC 4360 — extended communities, 8 octets and *typed*. You need these for route targets at D61, not for tagging | https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc4360 |
| 5 min | RFC 7999 — BLACKHOLE. One page, and it reappears at D33 and D86 | https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc7999 |

**Do — 50 min**

1. Work out the arithmetic yourself before reading anyone's explanation. A standard community is 32 bits, conventionally split `ASN:value` as 16+16. **A 4-byte ASN consumes the whole field.** There is nothing left for the value. That is the entire motivation for RFC 8092, and it is CP-26's answer.
2. Set a standard community on a route and confirm it arrives. **Then find the gotcha:** many implementations do not send communities to a neighbour unless told to. Find the knob (`send-community` or equivalent), turn it off, and confirm the community silently vanishes. Silent loss of policy signalling is a real outage class.
3. Set a large community. Confirm it survives an eBGP hop alongside the standard one.
4. **NO_EXPORT, live.** Tag a route with it, advertise to an eBGP neighbour, and confirm they accept it but do not pass it on. Then NO_ADVERTISE and confirm the difference. These two do real work in the D33 action-community set.
5. Set multiple communities on one route, then use a route-map to match *one* of them and act. Then delete one community without disturbing the others — additive vs replace semantics is where community policy usually breaks.
6. **Sketch your scheme.** Open [`docs/community-scheme.md`](../docs/community-scheme.md) and fill in anything you can already decide — the informational tags for customer/peer/transit are not hard, and having a draft before D32 turns that day into refinement instead of a blank page.

**Cheapest hands-on**
Step 2's disappearing community. Ten minutes, and it is the one that will otherwise cost you an evening at D34.

**Done when**
- [ ] The 4-byte-ASN arithmetic worked out unaided
- [ ] A community silently lost by disabling send-community, then recovered
- [ ] Standard and large communities both surviving an eBGP hop
- [ ] NO_EXPORT and NO_ADVERTISE demonstrated and distinguished
- [ ] Additive vs replace semantics tested
- [ ] Draft informational tags written into `docs/community-scheme.md`

**Cards touched** — CP-26

## B32 · Filtering, and RFC 8212

**Why this day exists**
CP-25 asks what RFC 8212 changed and what the old behaviour caused. The honest answer involves route leaks that took down large parts of the internet, and it is the reason D37 and D80 exist. This is also the day you learn AS-path regex, which you will need at D79 when bgpq4 generates filters for you and you have to read them.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* what a compliant BGP implementation does with an eBGP session that has no export policy, and what implementations did before RFC 8212.

**Source — 40 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 15 min | RFC 8212 — short. The default is now "announce nothing without explicit policy" | https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc8212 |
| 15 min | AS-path access lists and regex: `^$`, `^65001_`, `_65001$`, `.*`. Work out what each matches before checking | FRR docs · https://docs.frrouting.org/en/latest/bgp.html |
| 10 min | Order of operations — inbound: neighbour filters, then route-map; outbound the reverse. Get this wrong and your filter is decorative | vendor docs |

**Do — 50 min**

1. **Demonstrate 8212.** FRR enforces it by default (`bgp ebgp-requires-policy`). Bring up an eBGP session with no policy at all and confirm nothing is announced. Then disable the enforcement and watch everything leak. That before/after is the card.
2. Write the AS-path regex for "routes originated by my directly connected customer AS and nobody beyond" — `^65002$`. Test it against paths of length 1, 2 and 3. Then `^$`, which matches locally-originated routes only, and is how you build "announce only my own prefixes".
3. Build a customer-style inbound filter: accept only the prefixes that customer is authorised to announce, from a prefix-list, **and** verify the AS-path originates with them. Two independent checks. At D79–D80 this filter gets generated by bgpq4 from an AS-SET, and at D83 RPKI adds a third check — knowing why there are three is most of Block 6.
4. Announce something outside the filter and confirm the drop. **Then find where it was dropped** — `show bgp neighbor ... received-routes` versus the table shows whether the filter ran before or after the route was accepted. Knowing which side a policy runs on is what makes D41 debuggable.
5. Apply a max-prefix limit with a warning threshold below the teardown limit. Exceed the warning, confirm the log. Exceed the limit, confirm the session drops. **Then think about the operational trade-off** and write it down: a session that tears down on a leak protects you, and also takes your customer offline at 3am. D37 revisits this.
6. Add bogon filtering inbound. RFC 1918, documentation prefixes, default. Note that your own lab uses documentation prefixes, so your bogon list and your lab addressing are in direct conflict — resolve it deliberately and record how.

**Done when**
- [ ] 8212 behaviour demonstrated both ways, on and off
- [ ] AS-path regex tested against paths of three different lengths
- [ ] A two-check customer filter built: prefix-list *and* AS-path origin
- [ ] An out-of-filter announcement dropped, and the drop point located
- [ ] Max-prefix warning and teardown both triggered, trade-off written down
- [ ] Bogon list applied, and its conflict with your lab addressing resolved on purpose

**Cards touched** — CP-25

## B33 + B34 · Aggregation and multihoming

**Why this day exists**
Aggregation is how the DFZ stays a survivable size, and it is the mechanism multihoming depends on — which is why these two merged. CP-27 asks you to list every inbound load-sharing lever and rank them by how well they actually work, and the honest ranking is short and mostly disappointing.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* every lever available for influencing *inbound* traffic across two upstreams, ranked by effectiveness, with the reason the top one still fails.

**Source — 40 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 15 min | `aggregate-address`, `summary-only`, `suppress-map`, `unsuppress-map`. What ATOMIC_AGGREGATE and AS_SET are for, and why AS_SET is rarely used | FRR BGP docs |
| 15 min | Multihoming: the requirements — your own ASN, provider-independent space, and why you cannot multihome on a provider's PA assignment | APNIC · https://academy.apnic.net/en/course/introduction-to-bgp |
| 10 min | Why more-specifics work for inbound TE and why everyone hates that they work | any operational reference |

**Do — 50 min**

1. Aggregate four contiguous /24s into a /22. Confirm the aggregate appears and find ATOMIC_AGGREGATE in a capture. Then `summary-only` and confirm the components are suppressed.
2. `unsuppress-map` toward one neighbour only: the aggregate goes everywhere, one specific leaks to one neighbour. **That is a real inbound TE technique** — and it is also, at scale, how the routing table got to its current size.
3. Build the two-upstream topology: your AS, two transit providers, one prefix.
4. **Outbound first, because it works.** Local-pref by provider. Confirm every router in your AS exits the way you chose. Total control, entirely local.
5. **Now inbound, which does not.** Try each lever in turn and record what actually happened:
   - **Prepending** toward one provider. Effective, blunt, and their customers' local-pref beats it entirely.
   - **MED** — only meaningful between two links to the *same* provider. Useless across different ASes without `always-compare-med`, which nobody enables for you.
   - **More-specifics** — announce a /24 out of your /23 to one provider. **This works, reliably, which is the problem.** Note the cost you are imposing on every router in the DFZ, and that it is the reason RFC 7454 has opinions.
   - **Communities** — your provider's published action communities, if they offer them. Often the only lever that does what you meant, because it asks them to set local-pref on your behalf.
6. Rank them. Then write the sentence that explains why the ranking is disappointing: **outbound is a decision, inbound is a request.** You control your own path selection completely and someone else's not at all. That is CP-03, and it is the same conclusion you reached experimentally at B28 — arriving at it twice by different routes is why you will still have it in an interview in December.
7. Confirm the withdrawal case: pull one upstream and time how long until traffic actually shifts. That number is why D39's PIC exists.

**Done when**
- [ ] Aggregate built, ATOMIC_AGGREGATE located in a capture, `summary-only` verified
- [ ] `unsuppress-map` leaking one specific to one neighbour
- [ ] Outbound control demonstrated across the whole AS
- [ ] All four inbound levers tried, each with its observed limit recorded
- [ ] The ranking written, with the outbound-decision/inbound-request sentence
- [ ] Failover time measured on upstream withdrawal

**Cards touched** — CP-27, CP-03 (armed early)

## B35 · REVIEW + the three-AS topology

*Take the Saturday 180-minute slot — see the scheduling note. This is the block's deliverable and the prerequisite the core plan names explicitly.*

**Why this day exists**
The README marks B35 as the D22 prerequisite. It is more than that: this topology, with policy on it, is the thing you extend at D41, prove no-transit on at D42, and ship as **A3** — the flagship. Everything you build today you will build on for the next two months.

**Objective**
Three ASes, eBGP between them, iBGP inside at least one, and a basic policy framework that survives a stranger reading it.

**Source — 15 min**
None. Re-read [`docs/artifacts.md`](../docs/artifacts.md) A3's done-criteria instead. You are not building A3 today, but you are building its skeleton, and knowing what it becomes changes what you write down.

**Do — 150 min**

1. **Topology.** Three ASes with private ASNs (RFC 6996), documentation prefixes (RFC 3849/5737) per [`docs/addressing-plan.md`](../docs/addressing-plan.md). AS1 is yours and has at least three routers with an IGP and iBGP full mesh. AS2 and AS3 are external — treat one as a transit provider and one as a peer. Deploys clean from one command, configs in the repo.
2. **Ingress policy on every eBGP session.** Tag each route on arrival with a community identifying where it came from — customer, peer, or transit. Use the draft scheme you started at B31. Every route tagged; no untagged routes in the table.
3. **Local-pref by neighbour type.** Customer 200, peer 150, transit 100. Confirm from `show bgp` that the hierarchy is actually in effect, not just configured.
4. **Ingress filtering.** Prefix-list plus AS-path check per session, from B32. Bogons dropped. Max-prefix set with a warning threshold.
5. **Egress policy — the one that matters.** Announce your own prefixes to everyone. Announce your customer's prefixes to everyone. **Announce nothing learned from the peer to the transit, or from the transit to the peer.** Then *prove it*: advertise a distinctive prefix from AS2, and capture the advertisement going to AS3 showing it absent. That capture is the seed of A3's central piece of evidence, and D42's whole deliverable.
6. **Communities stripped on egress** to external neighbours. Verify from a capture that your internal tags are not leaving.
7. **REVIEW — closed book, out loud, standing up.** Then log every one in [`docs/checkpoints.md`](../docs/checkpoints.md):
   - CP-02 — path selection, in order, with tie-breakers. Second attempt; the card is due again anyway.
   - CP-22 — the iBGP gap, then the rule.
   - CP-23 — why `network` is SP-safe.
   - CP-24 — local-pref vs MED, direction and who can ignore which.
   - CP-25 — RFC 8212, before and after.
   - CP-26 — what forces large communities.
   - CP-27 — inbound levers, ranked.
8. Write a short README in `02-bgp-policy/` describing the design: the ASes, the policy layers, and the no-transit proof. Two hundred words. It becomes A3's README.

**Done when**
- [ ] Three-AS topology deploying clean from one command, configs in the repo
- [ ] Every eBGP-learned route tagged on ingress
- [ ] Local-pref hierarchy verified in the table, not just in config
- [ ] Ingress filters and max-prefix on every session
- [ ] **No-transit demonstrated with a capture**, not asserted
- [ ] Community stripping verified on egress from a capture
- [ ] All seven checkpoint cards attempted closed-book and logged honestly
- [ ] `02-bgp-policy/README.md` written

**Cards touched** — CP-02, CP-22, CP-23, CP-24, CP-25, CP-26, CP-27
