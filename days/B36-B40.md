Four sessions covering five original days — B36 folds into B37.

The last block of the bridge phase. It ends with **B40, the bridge capstone**: rebuild the B35 three-AS topology on vJunos, dual-stacked, from scratch, without notes. That day is the gate. Everything after it — the entire core plan — assumes you can stand up a multi-AS BGP topology on a vendor NOS without help.

Two threads converge here. IPv6 stops being an afterthought and becomes an addressing design you have to justify, which is D18's deliverable and [`docs/addressing-plan.md`](../docs/addressing-plan.md)'s empty hierarchy section. And Junos stops being something you read about and becomes the CLI you work in, because MX and PTX dominate service provider networks and D20, D21 and every policy day after assume it.

## B36 + B37 · IPv6, and a provider addressing plan

**Why this day exists**
D18 asks for an IPv6 addressing plan for your lab AS, *fully justified in writing*, and [`docs/addressing-plan.md`](../docs/addressing-plan.md) has a hierarchy section waiting for exactly that. CP-28 asks why /127 on point-to-point links, which has a real answer involving two specific attacks. Getting this right now means D18 is writing rather than deciding.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* why service providers put /127s on point-to-point links rather than /64s, naming both problems it avoids. And: what SLAAC cannot give you that DHCPv6 can, and vice versa.

**Source — 45 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 15 min | Address types and scope: global unicast, link-local, unique local, multicast. **Solicited-node multicast** — how it replaces ARP's broadcast | APNIC IPv6 Fundamentals · https://academy.apnic.net/en/course/ipv6-fundamentals/ |
| 10 min | NDP: NS, NA, RS, RA, and how DAD works. Note that a link-local address is mandatory and everything else is optional | RFC 4861 §4, skim |
| 10 min | RFC 6164 — /127 on inter-router links. **This is the objective; read it properly, it is short** | https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc6164 |
| 10 min | Provider allocation hierarchy: /32 to the ISP, /48 per site, /56 per small customer, /64 per LAN | APNIC, or your RIR's policy pages |

**Do — 45 min**

1. **NDP on the wire.** On any dual-stacked lab node: `tcpdump -ni <if> icmp6` and bring an interface up. Capture the router solicitation, the router advertisement, the neighbour solicitation, and duplicate address detection. Match each to what you read. There is no ARP here and no broadcast — find the solicited-node multicast address and work out how it is derived from the unicast address.
2. Watch SLAAC configure an address from an RA. Then find the flags in the RA — M and O — and work out what each instructs the host to do. **Now state what SLAAC cannot do:** it gives an address but not DNS, not a hostname, and no record of who has what. That last one is why enterprises use DHCPv6 and why providers mostly do not care.
3. Run DHCPv6 stateful on one segment. Note the DUID rather than a MAC, and that the lease is bound to it.
4. **The /127 argument, demonstrated.** RFC 6164 gives two reasons. The ping-pong problem: on a /64 point-to-point link, a packet to an unused address in that prefix bounces between the two routers until TTL expires — an amplification vector. And NDP cache exhaustion: an attacker scanning the /64 forces the router to hold neighbour state for addresses that do not exist. Say both out loud. **A /127 has exactly two usable addresses and neither attack has anywhere to go.**
5. **Now write the plan.** Open [`docs/addressing-plan.md`](../docs/addressing-plan.md) and fill in the IPv6 hierarchy section for your lab AS from `2001:db8::/32`. Decide and justify, in writing: the per-PoP allocation size and where the summarisation boundary sits; that loopbacks come from one contiguous /64 as /128s, and why never splitting that /64 matters; /127s on links, carved from a dedicated block so they are greppable; what a customer gets and why never a /64.
6. Cross-check against the IS-IS system-ID convention already in that file. If your loopback numbering does not make the system IDs readable in `show isis database`, change the numbering now — it is free today and annoying at D11.

**Cheapest hands-on**
Step 1. Twenty minutes of tcpdump, and NDP stops being a list of acronyms.

**Done when**
- [ ] NS, NA, RS, RA and DAD all identified in a real capture
- [ ] Solicited-node multicast address derived by hand from a unicast address
- [ ] The M and O flags found in an RA, and what each instructs
- [ ] Both /127 arguments stated unaided
- [ ] `docs/addressing-plan.md` IPv6 hierarchy section filled in, every level justified, **no TBD left**
- [ ] Loopback numbering cross-checked against the IS-IS system-ID convention

**Cards touched** — CP-28, CP-29

## B38 · Lab — dual-stack the three-AS topology

**Why this day exists**
This is where AFI/SAFI stops being a term and becomes a thing you configure — which is exactly what D22 opens the core plan's BGP block with. Every service carried in BGP for the rest of the plan (VPNv4 at D62, EVPN at D71, flowspec at D87) is another address family on a session shaped like the one you build today.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* what is shared and what is separate when one BGP session carries two address families — and what happens to an IPv6 route's next-hop when it is advertised over an IPv4 session.

**Source — 25 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 15 min | MP-BGP: the MP_REACH_NLRI and MP_UNREACH_NLRI attributes, and how AFI/SAFI selects what they carry | RFC 4760, §3 and §4 |
| 10 min | Per-address-family configuration and policy — one session, two RIBs, two sets of policy | FRR BGP docs · https://docs.frrouting.org/en/latest/bgp.html |

**Do — 65 min**

1. Add IPv6 to the B35 topology: /127s on every inter-router link, /128 loopbacks, from the plan you wrote yesterday. Use the plan — if it does not survive contact with the topology, fix the plan, not the addressing.
2. Bring up the IGP for IPv6 and confirm loopback-to-loopback reachability over v6 before touching BGP.
3. **Activate the IPv6 unicast address family on your existing IPv4 BGP sessions.** Capture the session restart and find the multiprotocol capability negotiation in the OPEN — you saw this field at B22 without needing it. Now it matters.
4. **The next-hop problem.** Advertise an IPv6 prefix over an IPv4 session. Look at the next-hop the receiving router installs. Depending on implementation you will get something unusable or something surprising, and the route will not be installed. Work out why: the next-hop must be reachable *in the address family of the route*. Fix it — either set the v6 next-hop explicitly, or run a separate BGP session over IPv6 transport.
5. **Do it both ways and compare**, because this is a real design decision providers argue about: one session carrying both families is fewer sessions and one shared fate; two sessions over their own transports means v4 and v6 fail independently. Write the trade-off down in two sentences. It comes back at D19 as multi-topology vs single-topology IS-IS — the same question in a different protocol.
6. Apply per-address-family policy: a route-map affecting v6 only, leaving v4 untouched. Confirm both. Policy that leaks across address families is a classic way to break half your internet.
7. Re-verify the B35 no-transit property **for IPv6 as well**. If your egress policy was written v4-only, you are now transiting between peers over v6 and would not know. Capture the proof for both families.
8. Commit to `02-bgp-policy/`.

**Done when**
- [ ] Topology dual-stacked from the written plan, IGP up for both families
- [ ] Multiprotocol capability located in an OPEN capture
- [ ] The IPv6-next-hop-over-IPv4-session failure observed, diagnosed, and fixed
- [ ] Both designs tried — shared session and separate sessions — with the trade-off written
- [ ] Per-address-family policy applied and confirmed not to leak
- [ ] **No-transit proven for IPv6 as well as IPv4**, both captures committed

**Cards touched** — CP-28 (exercised)

## B39 · Junos onboarding

**Why this day exists**
CP-30 asks what `commit confirmed` solves that `rollback` does not, and the answer is the reason Junos is trusted on production backbones. Tomorrow you rebuild everything in this CLI, so today is the last chance for the mechanics to be unfamiliar. D20, D21 and every core-plan policy day assume this fluency.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* what `commit confirmed` protects against that `rollback` cannot, naming the specific failure.

**Source — 30 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 15 min | Candidate vs active configuration. `set`, `delete`, `show \| compare`, `commit check`, `commit`, `rollback n` | https://www.juniper.net/documentation/ |
| 10 min | Configuration groups and `apply-groups` — inheritance, and why every SP config uses them | Juniper TechLibrary |
| 5 min | `deactivate` vs `delete`, and the rescue configuration | Juniper TechLibrary |

**Do — 60 min**

*If vJunos would not run on your hardware at B6, today is the day the fallback has to be real — not tomorrow.* Use the CML free tier or an hourly cloud VM. Do not attempt the capstone on a substitute NOS: the point of B40 is Junos fluency, and cEOS will not give you that.

1. Candidate config mechanics: make three unrelated changes, `show | compare`, and read the diff. Commit none of them, then `rollback 0` and confirm all three vanish together. **Atomic configuration is the first thing that makes this CLI different from IOS**, where every line takes effect as you type it.
2. `commit check` on a config with a deliberate error — reference an undefined policy. Confirm it fails without touching the running config.
3. **`commit confirmed`, the objective.** Configure something that will cut your own management access — a firewall filter, or shut the interface you are connected through. `commit confirmed 2`. Lose your session. Wait. **The router rolls back by itself and you get access back.**

   Now state the difference precisely: `rollback` requires you to still be able to reach the device. `commit confirmed` assumes you might not be. That is the failure it protects against — the change that severs the path you would have used to undo it. Every remote config change on a production backbone is made this way.
4. `rollback 1` through `rollback 5`, and `show | compare rollback 3`. Diffing against a historical config, not just reverting to it, is how you answer "what changed last Tuesday".
5. **Configuration groups.** Put your interface defaults, or your BGP neighbour defaults, in a group and `apply-groups` it. Confirm with `show configuration | display inheritance` that the inherited values are really there. Tomorrow you configure three ASes; groups are the difference between that fitting in the session and not.
6. `deactivate` a stanza, commit, confirm it is inert but still present. This is how you take a peer out of service without losing its configuration — and it is what you will want at D95 when killing things on purpose.
7. Write a one-page cheat sheet in `days/` or the journal: the ten commands you will need tomorrow. **You are allowed to use this at B40** — knowing the CLI is the point, memorising flag syntax is not.

**Done when**
- [ ] Three changes committed and rolled back atomically
- [ ] `commit check` failing on a deliberate error without affecting the running config
- [ ] **`commit confirmed` allowed to self-recover from a change that cut your own access**
- [ ] `show | compare rollback n` used to diff against history
- [ ] A configuration group applied and verified with `display inheritance`
- [ ] `deactivate` demonstrated
- [ ] Cheat sheet written

**Cards touched** — CP-30

## B40 · BRIDGE CAPSTONE — rebuild B35 on Junos, dual-stacked, from scratch

*Take the Saturday 180-minute slot. See the scheduling note — this day on a weekday evening is a wasted capstone.*

**Why this day exists**
The gate. Forty days of bridge phase exist to make the core plan survivable, and this is the only day that tests whether they worked. If it goes badly, the honest response is to repeat the weak part before starting D1 — that is what the day is *for*. Discovering the gap here costs three days; discovering it at D22 costs three weeks.

**Objective**
The B35 three-AS topology, dual-stacked, with full basic policy, rebuilt on Junos from nothing — and an honest list of what you could not do without looking up.

**What "no notes" means** — be precise, or the day tests nothing:

| Allowed | Not allowed |
|---|---|
| Vendor documentation for syntax | Your own previous configs, from any lab |
| Your B39 cheat sheet | The B35 or B38 topology files |
| The addressing plan in `docs/` | Copy-paste of anything you wrote before |
| RFCs | Asking a model for the config |

You are testing whether you know *what to build and why*, not whether you have memorised Junos syntax. Looking up `set protocols bgp group` is fine. Looking up whether you need next-hop-self is not — that is B25, and if it has gone, that is the finding.

**Source — 0 min**
None. That is the point.

**Do — 165 min**

1. **Design on paper first, 15 minutes.** Three ASes, ASNs and prefixes from the addressing plan, which routers, which sessions, which policy on which session. If you cannot draw it, stop and go back to B35 — that is a legitimate outcome of today.
2. Build the topology file and the Junos configs from scratch. Use configuration groups from B39; three ASes of per-neighbour config without them will not fit in the session.
3. Bring up the IGP, then iBGP inside your AS, then eBGP to the two external ASes. Dual-stacked throughout.
4. Apply the policy framework: ingress community tagging by neighbour type, local-pref hierarchy, ingress prefix and AS-path filtering, egress policy, community stripping at the boundary.
5. **Prove no-transit, for both address families.** Same evidence as B35 and B38 — advertise a distinctive prefix from one external AS, capture the advertisement to the other, show it absent. In `policy-statement` this time.
6. **The honest part.** As you go, keep a list of everything you had to look up and everything you got wrong. Do not clean it up afterwards. That list is the actual artifact of the day.
7. Commit everything to `02-bgp-policy/` alongside the FRR versions. **The same policy in two vendors' syntax, both working, both committed** is a portfolio piece in its own right and it is the shape of what D21 asks for with IOS-XR.
8. Journal: what took longest, what you could not remember, and — the question that decides your next three days — **is anything on that list load-bearing for D22?**

**Done when**
- [ ] Three-AS dual-stacked topology on Junos, deploying from committed configs
- [ ] Full policy framework applied in `policy-statement` form
- [ ] No-transit proven by capture, for IPv4 **and** IPv6
- [ ] Configuration groups used rather than repeated per-neighbour stanzas
- [ ] The look-it-up list written and **not** tidied
- [ ] Both vendors' versions of the same policy committed side by side
- [ ] A written decision: proceed to D1, or repeat a specific bridge day first

**Cards touched** — CP-02, CP-22, CP-23, CP-24, CP-25, CP-26, CP-28, CP-30. Re-log any that this day proved you had over-scored.
