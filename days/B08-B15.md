# B8–B15 · Link-state internals

Eight sessions on OSPF, in a plan whose backbone runs IS-IS. That is deliberate: you already know some OSPF, the mechanisms are the same ones IS-IS uses under different names, and at D14 you have to write the argument for choosing IS-IS. You cannot make that argument from a position of only knowing IS-IS.

**The trap in this block is reading.** Six of these eight days are conceptual, and a conceptual day with no terminal time is a day you will have forgotten by D9. Every card below has a **Cheapest hands-on** — ten minutes minimum, non-negotiable.

> **Merge note.** B14 is merged into B15 per [`docs/schedule.md`](../docs/schedule.md) — applied 2026-08-08. The combined card is at the end of this file.

---

## B8 · Link-state vs distance-vector · Dijkstra by hand

**Why this day exists**
Every convergence number you measure at D17, D54 and D95 decomposes into detection, SPF, and FIB update. Today you learn what SPF actually costs, by paying it yourself.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* what each router in a link-state domain holds that is identical across the area, and what each computes that is unique to itself.

**Source — 40 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 15 min | Distance-vector vs link-state — the count-to-infinity problem and what flooding a topology instead of a distance buys you | APNIC Routing Fundamentals, IGP module · https://academy.apnic.net/en/course/routing-fundamentals-course |
| 15 min | RFC 2328 §2 only — "The Topological Database". Twelve pages. Do not read further today | https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc2328#section-2 |
| 10 min | Dijkstra's algorithm — the tentative/permanent list mechanic, not the proof | any reference |

**Do — 50 min**

1. Draw a 6-node graph. Asymmetric weights on at least two links — symmetric graphs hide the interesting cases.
2. Run Dijkstra by hand, on paper, root at node A. Write the tentative list and the permanent list at **every** iteration. Do not shortcut to the answer you can see.
3. Now root at node D and do it again. Same database, different tree. **That is the objective — say it out loud now.**
4. Change one link weight. Re-run from A. Note how much of the tree changed, and how much did not. This is what "incremental SPF" at B12 is optimising.
5. Add a seventh node hanging off one edge. Note that every router in the area must now recompute, for a leaf that affects almost none of them. Hold that thought until D9 — it is half the argument for IS-IS levels and OSPF areas both.

**Cheapest hands-on**
Bring up the B5 two-node FRR lab and run `show ip ospf database` and `show ip ospf route`. You are looking at the database and the tree you just computed by hand.

**Done when**
- [ ] Two shortest-path trees produced by hand from one graph, iterations shown
- [ ] Can state what is common to all routers and what is per-router, unaided
- [ ] Photo or transcription of the working committed to `01-igp-core/`

**Cards touched** — CP-18

## B9 · OSPF adjacency — hello, DR/BDR, neighbour states

**Why this day exists**
CP-14 asks you to walk Down → Full and say what is exchanged at each state. It is a standard interview question, and at D13 you break the IS-IS equivalent, which has no DR and no ExStart.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* the neighbour states in order, and what is exchanged in each.

**Source — 45 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 25 min | RFC 2328 §7.2 and §10 — the neighbour state machine and the events that drive it | https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc2328#section-10 |
| 10 min | DR/BDR election — priority, then router ID; and why it is **non-preemptive** | RFC 2328 §7.3 |
| 10 min | The five OSPF packet types and what each carries | RFC 2328 §4.3 |

**Do — 45 min**

1. On the B5 lab, `debug ospf neighbor` (or FRR's equivalent) and bounce the interface. Watch the states scroll: Down → Init → 2-Way → ExStart → Exchange → Loading → Full.
2. Capture the adjacency forming: `tcpdump -ni <link> -w /tmp/adj.pcap proto ospf` while bouncing. Read it back with `-r` and identify, in order, the Hello, the DBD exchange, the LSR, the LSU, the LSAck. **Decode a real DBD packet** — find the MTU field, the I/M/MS bits, and the master/slave negotiation.
3. Convert the link to a broadcast segment (three FRR nodes on a bridge). Watch the DR election. Then bring up a fourth node with a *higher* priority and confirm it does **not** take over. Non-preemptive election surprises people in interviews.
4. Kill the DR. Time how long until the BDR takes over and the LSDB restabilises. Rough numbers are fine today; D17 is where measurement gets rigorous.
5. Write the state list from memory. Check it. Rewrite the ones you got wrong.

**Done when**
- [ ] All seven states observed live, in order
- [ ] A DBD packet decoded from a capture, MTU field located
- [ ] Non-preemptive DR election demonstrated with a higher-priority latecomer
- [ ] State list written from memory, unaided

**Cards touched** — CP-14 (first attempt today)

## B10 · LSA types 1–5 and 7

**Why this day exists**
CP-15 asks which LSAs cross an ABR, which cross an ASBR, and which never leave their area. This is also the day that makes B11's stub types comprehensible instead of memorised — every stub type is defined by which LSA it blocks.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* for each LSA type — who originates it, what it carries, and its flooding scope.

**Source — 40 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 30 min | RFC 2328 §12.4 — LSA origination, per type | https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc2328#section-12.4 |
| 10 min | Type 7 and NSSA — enough to know it exists; B11 covers the reasoning | RFC 3101 §1–2 |

**Do — 50 min**

1. Build the table before you look at anything. Six rows, four columns: type, originator, contents, scope. Fill in what you already know. Then correct it from the RFC in a different colour. **The corrections are your study list**, not the table.
2. On a lab with an ABR and an ASBR — extend B5 to four nodes, two areas, one redistributing a static route — dump `show ip ospf database` on each router. For each LSA present, name its type before reading the header.
3. Answer these three from the live database, not from theory:
   - Which LSA does a router in area 1 hold that describes a link in area 0? Which does it *not* hold?
   - How does a router in area 1 reach an external prefix it has no type 5 originator adjacency to? (Type 4 is the answer — find it in the database and say what it carries.)
   - Which LSA changed when you shut one interface, and how far did that change flood?
4. Question 3 is the important one. A type 1 change floods the whole area; a type 3 change crosses the ABR as a *different* LSA. Watch that translation happen.

**Done when**
- [ ] Table complete for types 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7 — originator, contents, scope
- [ ] Every row confirmed against a live LSDB, not just the RFC
- [ ] The type 4 question answered from a real database

**Cards touched** — CP-15

## B11 · Multi-area, ABR/ASBR, stub types

**Why this day exists**
Area design is the OSPF answer to the scaling problem you saw at B8 step 5. At D9 you meet the IS-IS answer, which is different in a way you can only appreciate having seen this one.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* for stub, totally-stubby, and NSSA — what is blocked, what is injected in its place, and what problem NSSA exists to solve.

**Source — 40 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 20 min | RFC 2328 §3 — area structure, the backbone, ABR behaviour, virtual links | https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc2328#section-3 |
| 20 min | Stub area types, one comparison table | Day One: Routing the IP, ch. 4 · https://www.juniper.net/us/en/training/jnbooks/day-one/ |

**Do — 50 min**

1. Build the comparison table yourself: rows = normal / stub / totally-stubby / NSSA / totally-NSSA; columns = type 3 allowed? type 4? type 5? type 7? default injected?
2. On the four-node lab, make area 1 stub. `show ip ospf database` in area 1 before and after. Diff them. The default route that appeared — find which LSA type carries it and who originated it.
3. Make it totally-stubby. Diff again. Count the LSAs removed. That count is the entire argument for stub areas.
4. Now put an ASBR *inside* area 1 and try to redistribute. It fails. **That failure is why NSSA exists** — convert to NSSA and watch the type 7 appear, then find the type 5 it becomes at the ABR. Confirm the translation with the forwarding address.
5. Note in the journal: which of these five options a service provider backbone actually uses, and why the answer is usually "none of them, we run IS-IS". Save the reasoning for D14.

**Done when**
- [ ] Comparison table complete and confirmed against live LSDBs
- [ ] LSA count in area 1 recorded for normal / stub / totally-stubby
- [ ] Type 7 → type 5 translation observed at the ABR

**Cards touched** — CP-16

## B12 · SPF timers, LSA throttling, incremental SPF, reference bandwidth

**Why this day exists**
This is the first day about **stability under failure** rather than correctness. Every timer here has an IS-IS equivalent at D15, and D15 assumes you already understand exponential backoff.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* why SPF and LSA generation are throttled with exponential backoff rather than a fixed delay, and what a flapping link does to a domain without it.

**Source — 40 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 20 min | SPF and LSA throttle timers — the initial / increment / maximum triple and how the three interact | FRR OSPF docs, timers section · https://docs.frrouting.org/en/latest/ospfd.html |
| 10 min | Reference bandwidth and cost calculation | RFC 2328 §11.1 plus vendor defaults |
| 10 min | Incremental SPF — what it recomputes and what it skips | any vendor whitepaper |

**Do — 50 min**

1. Find the default SPF throttle triple on FRR. Write down what each of the three numbers means before changing anything.
2. Flap a link on a timer: `while true; do ip link set <if> down; sleep 2; ip link set <if> up; sleep 2; done`. Watch SPF run counts (`show ip ospf` has the counters). Note how the backoff stretches the interval as the flapping continues.
3. Set the timers aggressively low. Flap again. Watch CPU. **You have just built the failure mode that throttling prevents** — write down what it looks like, because at D15 you will be asked to tune IS-IS timers and the temptation will be to set everything to zero.
4. Reference bandwidth: change it on **one** router only in a multi-area lab. Find the asymmetric routing it causes. This is CP-17 and it is a favourite interview question because the failure is silent — routes still work, they just take stupid paths.
5. Set it consistently everywhere and confirm the paths correct themselves.

**Done when**
- [ ] Default throttle triple recorded, each number explained
- [ ] Aggressive timers demonstrated causing measurable instability
- [ ] Asymmetric path caused by a single-router reference bandwidth change, and located in `show ip route`

**Cards touched** — CP-17

## B13 · Lab — multi-area OSPF, 6 nodes, partition area 0

*Put this on the weekend slot if you can. It is the block's build day.*

**Why this day exists**
The block's deliverable. It is also a dry run for D11 — same node count, same verification discipline, different protocol.

**Objective**
A six-node, three-area OSPF domain that deploys from one command, and a written account of what happens when area 0 is cut in half.

**Source — 20 min**
No new reading. Re-read your own B10 and B11 tables. If you cannot rebuild them from memory first, that is the day's real finding.

**Do — 130 min**

1. Six nodes, three areas: area 0 with three routers, areas 1 and 2 hanging off separate ABRs. Topology committed to `01-igp-core/`, deploys clean from `containerlab deploy` with no manual steps afterwards. **Startup configs in the repo, not typed in by hand** — this is the standard `docs/artifacts.md` sets for A2, and starting it now costs nothing.
2. Verify the LSDB **per area**, by hand: for each area, list the LSA types present and predict the count before running the command. Where prediction and reality differ, that is a gap in B10 — record it.
3. Confirm full reachability, loopback to loopback, all six.
4. **Partition area 0.** Break the backbone into two halves that can still reach each other only via area 1. Predict what happens first. Then observe: inter-area routes disappear, because an ABR will not advertise between non-backbone areas.
5. Fix it with a virtual link. Confirm reachability returns. Then write two sentences on why virtual links are considered a design smell rather than a tool — you are documenting a thing you should never need in a network you designed.
6. Take a baseline convergence measurement: fail one core link under a steady ping, count lost packets, convert to milliseconds. Crude, and that is fine — put it in `docs/convergence-results.md` labelled as an OSPF baseline. **D17 will make this rigorous, and having a crude number to compare against is worth more than starting cold.**

**Done when**
- [ ] Six-node three-area topology in `01-igp-core/`, deploys clean, configs in the repo
- [ ] LSDB verified per area with predictions recorded against reality
- [ ] Area 0 partitioned, symptom observed, virtual link fix applied
- [ ] One crude convergence number in `docs/convergence-results.md` with the method stated

**Cards touched** — CP-15, CP-16, CP-18 (all re-armed by use)

## B14 + B15 · OSPFv3, dual-stack, and block REVIEW

*Merged per `docs/schedule.md` merge #1. Deliberately light — if you have slipped, this is where you catch up.*

**Why this day exists**
OSPFv3 at 7 years' experience is a delta, not a subject: same machine, different transport, LSAs renamed and address information pulled out of the topology LSAs. Twenty minutes of reading. The rest of the session is the review that decides whether this block actually stuck.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* LSDB synchronisation, end to end, from memory. Plus: what OSPFv3 changed and why address-family independence needed a new protocol version rather than an extension.

**Source — 30 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 20 min | OSPFv3 delta from OSPFv2 — per-link not per-subnet, LSA renaming, link-local transport, the Instance ID | Day One: Routing the IP, ch. 4–5 · https://www.juniper.net/documentation/en_US/day-one-books/DO_Routing_the_IP.pdf |
| 10 min | RFC 5340 §2 — "Differences from OSPF for IPv4" | https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc5340#section-2 |

**Do — 60 min**

*Part one — 25 min, hands on:* dual-stack the B13 topology. OSPFv3 alongside OSPFv2, same areas. Confirm two separate LSDBs and two separate SPF computations for one physical topology. **Hold this observation.** At D19 you choose between multi-topology and single-topology IS-IS, and this is the shape of what the alternative looks like.

*Part two — 35 min, closed book, out loud, standing up:*

1. **LSDB synchronisation, end to end.** Two routers meet on a link with nothing in common. Narrate every step to full synchronisation, naming packet types. This is the block's headline objective.
2. LSA types 1–5 and 7 — originator, contents, scope. No notes.
3. Stub / totally-stubby / NSSA — blocked, injected, why NSSA exists.
4. Reference bandwidth changed on one router — what breaks, and why it is hard to spot.
5. Dijkstra: given a 6-node graph you have not seen, produce the tree.

Log every one in `docs/checkpoints.md` — CP-14 through CP-18. **`n` for anything you hesitated on.** A generous `y` today is a card that never gets re-asked and a gap you find in an interview instead.

**Done when**
- [ ] B13 topology dual-stacked, two LSDBs confirmed separate
- [ ] All five questions attempted out loud, closed book
- [ ] Five rows in the checkpoint answer log, honestly scored
- [ ] Any card scored `n` has its gap written specifically — "forgot what Loading state exchanges", not "shaky on states"
