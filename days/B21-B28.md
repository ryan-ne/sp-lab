# B21–B28 · BGP, first half

Six sessions covering eight original days — B21 folds into B22 and B26 into B27, per the merges in [`docs/schedule.md`](../docs/schedule.md).

This is the first half of the bridge phase's largest block. It ends at B28 with the attributes in place; **B29 is where you start writing path selection from memory**, and everything from D22 onward assumes you can. The split is deliberate: B21–B28 is *how BGP works*, B29–B35 is *how you control it*.

**The block's real risk is confidence.** Seven years of enterprise networking means you have configured BGP. Almost none of that experience covers what happens at an AS boundary you own, and the plan's whole second half depends on this half being solid rather than familiar. When a card says *say it out loud*, that is the test — not whether the lab came up.

> **Written ahead of schedule** — before B8–B15 and B16–B20 ran, so no feedback from either is folded in. **Review at the B16–B20 retro**, particularly B21+B22, which assumes BGP fundamentals are revision for you rather than new.

## B21 + B22 · What BGP is for, and how a session works

**Why this day exists**
Every remaining protocol in this plan is carried inside BGP. L3VPN at D61, EVPN at D71, flowspec at D87, SR-TE colour at D57 — all of them are address families on the session you build today. The FSM matters because at D38 you harden it and at D95 you kill it on purpose.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* why BGP runs over TCP when OSPF and IS-IS do not, and name one thing that choice gives you and one it costs you.

**Source — 45 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 10 min | Path vector vs link-state — what an AS_PATH is *for*, beyond loop prevention. Skim only; at your level this is revision | APNIC Introduction to BGP · https://academy.apnic.net/en/course/introduction-to-bgp |
| 20 min | RFC 4271 §8 — the finite state machine. Idle, Connect, Active, OpenSent, OpenConfirm, Established | https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc4271#section-8 |
| 15 min | RFC 4271 §4 — message formats. OPEN, UPDATE, NOTIFICATION, KEEPALIVE, and the capability negotiation inside OPEN | https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc4271#section-4 |

**Do — 45 min**

1. Write the six FSM states in order from memory after reading. Then the two that indicate trouble — **`Active` is the misleading one**; a session stuck in Active is not busy, it is failing to connect outbound. Write down what actually causes it.
2. Bring up an eBGP session between two FRR nodes. `show bgp summary` and watch it reach Established.
3. **Capture the OPEN.** `tcpdump -ni <link> -w /tmp/open.pcap port 179`, then clear the session. Read it back and find, inside the OPEN: the AS number, the hold time, the BGP identifier, and the **capabilities list**. Name each capability advertised. Multiprotocol and 4-octet-ASN will be there; those two are the reason D22's AFI/SAFI discussion works at all.
4. Force a NOTIFICATION: set the wrong remote-as on one side. Capture it, decode the error code and subcode, and confirm the session drops rather than retries silently. Now you know what a real misconfiguration looks like on the wire.
5. Set the hold time to 9 seconds on one side and 180 on the other. Which value wins? Confirm from the capture, not from documentation — negotiated hold time is a favourite interview detail.
6. Kill the link without clearing the session. Time how long until the session drops. That gap — hold timer, not link state — is exactly what BFD at D17 and GTSM at D38 exist to change.

**Done when**
- [ ] Six FSM states written from memory, with the specific cause of a stuck `Active`
- [ ] An OPEN decoded from a capture, every advertised capability named
- [ ] A NOTIFICATION forced and its error code read
- [ ] Negotiated hold time confirmed from the wire
- [ ] Session-drop time on link failure measured

**Cards touched** — none directly. The FSM is the setup for CP-22 at B24.

## B23 · Lab — first eBGP session, and the UPDATE on the wire

**Why this day exists**
The UPDATE message is the object every remaining BGP day manipulates. Communities at B31, local-pref at B28, MED, AS_PATH prepending at B27 — all of them are fields in a packet you decode today. Seeing it once, properly, means the rest are edits rather than abstractions.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* the three well-known mandatory path attributes, and what an UPDATE containing only withdrawn routes looks like.

**Source — 25 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 15 min | RFC 4271 §4.3 — UPDATE format: withdrawn routes, path attributes, NLRI. Note the attribute flags: optional, transitive, partial, extended-length | https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc4271#section-4.3 |
| 10 min | Well-known mandatory vs well-known discretionary vs optional transitive vs optional non-transitive | RFC 4271 §5 |

**Do — 65 min**

1. Two-node eBGP, different AS numbers, loopback on each.
2. Advertise the loopback with a `network` statement. **It will not appear until the exact prefix is in the RIB** — that requirement is not a quirk, it is the whole reason `network` is SP-safe, and B26+B27 explains why. Note the failure now.
3. `tcpdump -w`, then clear the session and capture the full convergence. In the capture, find in order: OPEN, KEEPALIVE, the first UPDATE, subsequent KEEPALIVEs.
4. **Decode one UPDATE completely.** Find and name: ORIGIN and its value (`i`, `e`, or `?` — and which one a `network` statement produces), AS_PATH and its segment type, NEXT_HOP and what address it actually holds, then the NLRI itself. Write the field list in the journal.
5. Withdraw the route — remove the `network` statement — and capture again. **An UPDATE with withdrawn routes and no NLRI.** Confirm the path attribute section is empty. This asymmetry surprises people.
6. Add a second prefix and confirm both travel in one UPDATE when their attributes match, and separate UPDATEs when they do not. That packing behaviour is why attribute diversity costs memory at D40.
7. Commit the topology to `02-bgp-policy/`. It is the seed of A3.

**Done when**
- [ ] eBGP session up, loopback advertised and received
- [ ] The `network`-statement-needs-an-exact-RIB-match failure observed deliberately
- [ ] One UPDATE fully decoded, every field named in the journal
- [ ] A withdrawal captured and its structure described
- [ ] Two prefixes shown sharing one UPDATE, then split by differing attributes
- [ ] Topology committed to `02-bgp-policy/`

**Cards touched** — CP-23 (armed)

## B24 · eBGP vs iBGP — the split-horizon rule

**Why this day exists**
CP-22 asks you to state the iBGP loop-prevention gap *precisely*. Vague answers are the norm here and they do not survive an interview. This rule is also the entire reason route reflectors exist at D29, and RR path hiding at D30 is a direct consequence.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* exactly why an iBGP-learned route is not re-advertised to another iBGP peer, naming the mechanism that is missing.

**Source — 35 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 20 min | RFC 4271 §9.2 — the update-send process and the iBGP restriction | https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc4271#section-9.2 |
| 15 min | What changes and what does not across an eBGP hop vs an iBGP hop: AS_PATH, NEXT_HOP, LOCAL_PREF, MED | any BGP reference |

**Do — 55 min**

1. Build the argument on paper before touching a lab. AS_PATH is BGP's loop prevention: a router rejects any route whose AS_PATH already contains its own AS. Now ask the question that matters — **what happens to AS_PATH when a route crosses an iBGP session?** Nothing. It is unchanged.
2. Therefore, inside an AS, AS_PATH cannot detect a loop, because every router in the AS shares one AS number and no hop adds anything. **That is the gap.** The protocol's answer is a blunt rule: never re-advertise an iBGP-learned route to another iBGP peer. Say it out loud in that order — gap first, rule second. A rule stated without its gap is memorisation.
3. Work out the consequence yourself: if no router relays, every router must hear it directly. That is the full mesh, and its cost is n(n−1)/2 sessions. Compute that for 5, 20 and 100 routers. The last number is why D29 exists.
4. Build the four-attribute table: for AS_PATH, NEXT_HOP, LOCAL_PREF and MED, does it survive an eBGP hop, and an iBGP hop? Fill it from reasoning, then verify against captures in tomorrow's lab. Two of these rows are B28's objective.
5. Predict what will break in the B25 lab: a route learned by one border router, present on a second, and unusable. Write down why before you build it.

**Cheapest hands-on**
On the B23 lab, add a third router as an iBGP peer of one side and confirm the eBGP-learned route *does* reach it — one iBGP hop is allowed. It is the second hop that stops. Fifteen minutes, and it makes the rule concrete.

**Done when**
- [ ] The gap stated before the rule, unaided
- [ ] Full-mesh session counts computed for 5, 20 and 100 routers
- [ ] Four-attribute survival table filled in from reasoning
- [ ] A written prediction of what B25 will break

**Cards touched** — CP-22

## B25 · Lab — iBGP, next-hop-self, and the IGP underneath

*Take the Saturday 180-minute slot — see the scheduling note.*

**Why this day exists**
The block's build day, and the first time the interaction between BGP and the IGP bites. D26 and D27 in the core plan are this lab again at carrier scale, and D27 explicitly asks you to fix next-hop resolution three ways and compare. Do all three today and D27 becomes a review.

**Objective**
A working iBGP full mesh where an externally-learned prefix is reachable from every router, and a written comparison of three ways to make the next-hop resolvable.

**Source — 20 min**
No new reading. Re-read your B24 prediction and the four-attribute table. If you cannot restate why the next-hop is about to be a problem, that is today's first finding.

**Do — 150 min**

1. Four routers in one AS, full-mesh iBGP **between loopbacks**, plus one eBGP session from `r1` to an external AS. Startup configs in the repo, deploying clean from one command — the A2 standard from [`docs/artifacts.md`](../docs/artifacts.md).
2. **Watch the sessions fail first.** With no IGP, the loopbacks are unreachable, so the iBGP sessions never establish. This is worth seeing before you fix it: BGP is an application, and it needs someone else to provide reachability. Bring up IS-IS or OSPF and watch them come up.
3. Now the real problem. The eBGP route reaches `r4` via iBGP, but check `show bgp` versus `show ip route` — **present in the BGP table, not installed in the RIB**, because NEXT_HOP is the external neighbour's address and no router other than `r1` has a route to it. Capture this state. It is the single most common BGP misunderstanding.
4. **Fix one — `next-hop-self` on r1.** Confirm the next-hop is rewritten to r1's loopback, which the IGP already carries. Re-check the RIB.
5. **Fix two — carry the eBGP link subnet in the IGP.** Revert fix one first. This also works. Then write down why service providers avoid it: you are injecting an external-facing subnet into your IGP, growing the LSDB with something that has no business there, and coupling your IGP to a neighbour's addressing.
6. **Fix three — a static route to the peer subnet on each router**, or an IGP `passive` interface. Works, does not scale, and now you can say why.
7. Compare all three in a table: what changes, what it costs, what it couples. **That table is the D27 deliverable, four weeks early.**
8. Verify the split-horizon rule directly: confirm `r4` does *not* re-advertise the iBGP-learned route to `r2` or `r3`. Then count the sessions you configured and check it against your B24 arithmetic.
9. Commit topology, configs and the comparison table to `02-bgp-policy/`.

**Done when**
- [ ] iBGP sessions observed failing without an IGP, then establishing with one
- [ ] The route-present-but-not-installed state captured before fixing it
- [ ] All three next-hop fixes applied, each verified, each reverted before the next
- [ ] Three-way comparison table written and committed
- [ ] Split-horizon confirmed by observation, session count matching B24's arithmetic

**Cards touched** — CP-22 (exercised)

## B26 + B27 · Origination, and the path attributes

**Why this day exists**
CP-23 asks why `network` is the SP-safe way to originate. The answer is a real operational argument — it is the difference between an outage contained to one prefix and an outage that leaks your entire IGP into the DFZ. This is also where AS_PATH stops being loop prevention and starts being a traffic-engineering lever, which is B28's setup and D36's whole subject.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* why `network` is safer than `redistribute` for originating a prefix into BGP, in terms of what happens when something goes wrong.

**Source — 40 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 15 min | `network` vs `redistribute` vs `aggregate-address` — the three ways to originate, and what each depends on | FRR BGP docs · https://docs.frrouting.org/en/latest/bgp.html |
| 15 min | ORIGIN, AS_PATH, NEXT_HOP in detail. AS_PATH segment types: AS_SEQUENCE vs AS_SET | RFC 4271 §5.1.1–5.1.3 |
| 10 min | AS_PATH prepending — the mechanism, and why its effect is advisory | any reference; the *why* is D36 |

**Do — 50 min**

1. Originate the same prefix three ways on the B25 lab — `network`, `redistribute static`, and `aggregate-address` — one at a time. Compare the ORIGIN code each produces. One of them gives `?` (incomplete), and that value follows the route across the entire internet.
2. **The safety experiment.** With `network`, delete the underlying route. The advertisement stops — one prefix, contained. Now switch to `redistribute connected` and add an interface. **Something you never intended to advertise is now in BGP.** That asymmetry is the whole answer to CP-23: `network` fails closed and requires an explicit statement per prefix; `redistribute` fails open and advertises whatever the source protocol happens to contain.
3. Then scale the thought: `redistribute ospf` on a router carrying a real IGP table, pointed at a transit provider. Write down, in one sentence, what you have just done to the internet. This is a real outage class, not a hypothetical.
4. `aggregate-address` with and without `summary-only`. Confirm the components disappear in one case and not the other, and find the ATOMIC_AGGREGATE attribute in a capture. B33 goes deeper; today just see it.
5. **Prepending.** Prepend your AS three times on advertisement and confirm the AS_PATH length at the neighbour. Then prepend on the *receiving* side with a route-map and note that it works too — and that only one of these is visible to anyone else.
6. Confirm loop prevention directly: try to advertise a route back into the AS that originated it. Capture the rejection. `show bgp neighbor ... received-routes` versus what is in the table shows where it was dropped.
7. Record the ORIGIN code produced by each of the three origination methods in the journal. It comes back at D24.

**Cheapest hands-on**
Step 2 alone. Fifteen minutes, and it is the card.

**Done when**
- [ ] Three origination methods tried, ORIGIN code recorded for each
- [ ] The fail-closed vs fail-open difference demonstrated, not just described
- [ ] `summary-only` behaviour observed and ATOMIC_AGGREGATE located in a capture
- [ ] Prepending applied on both send and receive, with the visibility difference noted
- [ ] A route rejected by AS_PATH loop prevention, captured

**Cards touched** — CP-23

## B28 · Local-preference and MED

**Why this day exists**
These two attributes are the whole of basic traffic engineering, and CP-24 asks which direction each influences plus which one a neighbour can ignore. The asymmetry between them is the seed of CP-03 — why inbound TE is fundamentally weaker than outbound — which is one of the ten questions and one of the best answers you can give in an SP interview.

**Objective**
*Say out loud:* which direction of traffic LOCAL_PREF controls and which MED controls, and which of the two your neighbour is free to ignore.

**Source — 35 min**

| Time | What | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 15 min | LOCAL_PREF — well-known discretionary, **never sent to eBGP peers**, higher wins | RFC 4271 §5.1.5 |
| 15 min | MED — optional non-transitive, sent to eBGP peers but not propagated onward, **lower wins** | RFC 4271 §5.1.4 |
| 5 min | `always-compare-med` and `deterministic-med` — what problem each solves | vendor docs |

**Do — 55 min**

1. **Prove the scope of LOCAL_PREF from a capture, not from documentation.** Set it on a route at `r1`, confirm every iBGP router sees it, then capture the eBGP advertisement leaving the AS and confirm the attribute is *absent*. Seeing the absence is the lesson.
2. Set different local-preferences for the same prefix learned from two different external neighbours. Watch every router in the AS choose the same exit. **That is outbound TE, and it works because the decision is entirely yours.**
3. Now MED. Advertise the same prefix to one neighbour with two different MEDs on two links. Confirm it influences which link they use to reach you — **inbound**. Then note the two things that make it weak: it is only compared between routes from the *same* neighbouring AS by default, and your neighbour can strip or override it with local-pref, which beats MED in path selection.
4. **Demonstrate the override.** On the neighbouring AS, set local-pref to defeat your MED preference. Your inbound engineering evaporates and you cannot tell. Write down what just happened — this is CP-03's answer, arrived at by experiment two months before the card is due.
5. Enable `always-compare-med` and observe the changed behaviour with routes from different ASes. Note why it is off by default and why turning it on can cause non-deterministic selection without `deterministic-med`.
6. Complete the four-attribute survival table from B24 with observed results, and correct anything you reasoned wrong.

**Cheapest hands-on**
Steps 1 and 4. The capture proving LOCAL_PREF never leaves, and the neighbour silently overriding your MED.

**Done when**
- [ ] LOCAL_PREF proven absent from an eBGP advertisement, from a capture
- [ ] Outbound exit selection changed across the whole AS with local-pref
- [ ] MED shown influencing a neighbour's inbound choice
- [ ] The neighbour's local-pref shown defeating your MED, with the consequence written down
- [ ] B24's survival table completed with observed values and corrections marked

**Cards touched** — CP-24
